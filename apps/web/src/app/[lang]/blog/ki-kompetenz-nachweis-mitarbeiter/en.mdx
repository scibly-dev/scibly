Proof of AI competence documents who in a company was trained when and on what topic in the safe and appropriate use of AI systems. No specific certificate is required, just solid documentation: a participant list, the training content, the date, and ideally evidence that the content was actually understood, not just attended.

## What "proof" means in practice

Proof isn't a single document, it's a chain of three components:

| Component     | What it shows                                              | Typical format                                                           |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Attendance    | Who was trained when                                       | Completion list from the LMS or a sign-off sheet                         |
| Content       | Exactly what was taught                                    | Course description, learning objectives, slide deck, or course recording |
| Understanding | Whether the content actually landed, not just was consumed | Knowledge check or final test with a result                              |

Without the third point, proof is vulnerable to challenge: a pure attendance list shows that someone was in the room or started a video, not that the person understood the content. That's exactly the distinction that typically gets questioned in an audit or review.

## Why this matters more now

The Article 4 AI literacy obligation from the EU AI Act (see [EU AI Act: The AI Literacy Obligation Explained](/blog/eu-ai-act-ki-kompetenz-pflicht)) makes documentation more than just a good idea. Without proof, in a dispute it can't be shown that a company met its obligation, even if training actually happened. The proof is therefore just as important as the training itself.

## How an LMS simplifies record-keeping

Manual record-keeping through spreadsheets and collected PDF certificates becomes impractical and error-prone past a certain company size. An LMS with automatic tracking solves this structurally: every completed training is automatically documented with date, content, and test result, searchable in case of a review. Scibly courses include knowledge checks directly within the course flow by default, not just a final test, so the record automatically shows more than mere attendance.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a certificate of attendance enough as proof of AI competence?

A pure attendance certificate only shows that someone attended the training, not that the content was understood. A combination of attendance, documented learning content, and a test result is more solid.

### How long should training records be kept?

There's no definitively settled general legal retention period specifically for AI competence records. In practice, many companies follow their existing retention periods for compliance training. When in doubt, involve your own legal department rather than guessing a deadline.

### Does every department need different training?

A sensible approach is a base training for everyone who uses AI tools, plus deeper content for higher-risk roles, such as people using AI output for customer communication or decisions. That can also show up in the records: different courses for different roles.

### What happens if a company can't produce proof?

That depends on the specific context, such as a regulatory review or an internal audit, and isn't a question with one universal answer. As a general rule: without documentation, it's hard to prove after the fact that training took place, even if it actually did.
