Language-learning apps like Duolingo are a revealing comparison case for employee training, because they show a familiar pattern: they're strong at building receptive knowledge, such as recognizing vocabulary or understanding simple sentences, but noticeably weaker at teaching situational and pragmatic competence, meaning reacting appropriately on the spot in a real conversation. That exact distinction explains why many employees successfully complete a training program and still can't act confidently in day-to-day work.

## Receptive knowledge isn't the same as applied competence

Receptive knowledge means recognizing or reproducing a piece of information when it's asked for. Situational competence means independently finding the right reaction in a real, often unpredictable situation. A compliance course can reliably teach receptive knowledge, such as "which rule applies here," without the person actually acting confidently in practice when a real edge case comes up that didn't appear exactly that way in the course.

## Why this distinction matters especially for corporate training

| Type of training                          | Mostly receptive knowledge | Mostly situational competence                         |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Fact-based compliance rules               | Yes, usually easy to teach | Less relevant                                         |
| Recognizing a phishing attempt            | Partly                     | Yes, real emails vary a lot from the practice example |
| A customer conversation about a complaint | Barely                     | Yes, every situation is somewhat different            |
| Operating a fixed software feature        | Yes                        | Barely                                                |

Most compliance and process training sits closer to the receptive end, while many soft-skill and customer-facing topics sit much closer to the situational end. A course format that works well for factual knowledge, such as pure knowledge quizzes, doesn't automatically transfer to topics that demand genuine situational reaction.

## How to improve transfer to day-to-day work

The most important lever is varying the practice situations, not repeating the same situation. If you only practice a single example of "how do I recognize a phishing email," that knowledge often transfers poorly to a real email that looks different from the practice example. Several, slightly different practice cases, ideally spread out over a longer period instead of all at once, improve actual transfer far more reliably than a single, perfectly matched practice task.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why isn't a passed final test proof of genuine competence?

Because a final test often tests receptive knowledge, meaning whether a piece of information can be recognized or recalled, but not whether the person reacts appropriately in a new, slightly different real situation.

### How can situational competence even be trained?

Through exercise formats that capture real variation, such as several different case studies or roleplays, instead of a single standard example that's always tested the same way. See also [AI Roleplay in Training](/blog/ki-rollenspiele-training).

### Does this problem affect knowledge training or behavior training more?

Mainly behavior training, where reacting in real, variable situations is the point. Pure knowledge training on fixed facts is less affected; receptive knowledge is often already sufficient there.

### What does this have to do with spaced repetition?

Spaced repetition mainly improves how long receptive knowledge stays in memory. For situational competence, varying the practice situations is additionally needed; repetition alone isn't enough for that.
