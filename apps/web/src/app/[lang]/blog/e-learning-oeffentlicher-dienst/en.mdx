The public sector faces a double challenge: a large share of the workforce will retire in the coming years, while new digital administrative procedures need to be rolled out at the same time. E-learning doesn't solve this problem on its own, but it makes onboarding and mandatory training scalable, in a situation where fewer staff need to pass on more knowledge at once.

## Why traditional onboarding is hitting its limits

In many government offices, knowledge transfer still happens informally: experienced colleagues verbally explain to new staff how a process is handled. That works as long as enough experienced staff have time for it. The retirement wave is shrinking exactly that capacity, while more new and career-changing employees need onboarding at the same time, because open positions are harder to fill. The result is a structural bottleneck: fewer people able to pass on knowledge, with rising demand at the same time.

## Where e-learning specifically helps in government administration

| Use case                                                   | Benefit                                                                                               |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Onboarding new administrative staff                        | Standardized onboarding across departments, regardless of who currently has capacity                  |
| Preserving knowledge before retirements                    | Experience is documented and turned into a usable course before it's lost                             |
| Mandatory training (data protection, information security) | Automatically trackable instead of manual sign-off sheets                                             |
| New administrative procedures (OZG-driven)                 | Practical training alongside the technical rollout, see [OZG Training](/blog/ozg-schulung-verwaltung) |

## The difference from the private sector

Government bodies operate under different conditions than private companies, which need to be factored into tool selection: procurement law limits how quickly and at what scale a new tool can be introduced, and data protection and information security requirements are often more strictly regulated than in a private-sector context. A pilot project that stays below the relevant state's direct-award threshold, on the other hand, can often be started without a lengthy tender process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is e-learning suitable for every area of government administration?

For standardizable knowledge such as mandatory training, process instructions, or onboarding content, e-learning works well. Complex discretionary decisions or highly situation-dependent tasks, on the other hand, can rarely be fully taught through e-learning; personal guidance remains important there.

### How can experience be preserved before a retirement?

An interview or a structured handover with the departing person, whose content is then turned into a course format, is a common approach. It's important to start early, not just in the final weeks before departure.

### What role does procurement law play in introducing an LMS?

Larger procurements must go through a tender process; smaller pilot projects, depending on the state and municipality, may fall below a direct-award threshold and can therefore start faster. The exact threshold varies by municipality and should be verified before committing.

### What sets an LMS for municipalities apart from a typical corporate LMS?

Little in terms of content, but quite a bit on the technical and procurement side: data protection and information security documentation is often reviewed more strictly, and the offering needs to fit municipal procurement realities, for example through a pilot model that stays below the direct-award threshold.
