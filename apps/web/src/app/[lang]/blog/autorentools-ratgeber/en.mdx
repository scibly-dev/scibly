Most L&D teams never really chose their authoring tool — they just used whatever someone already knew, whatever came bundled with the LMS, or whatever the previous person left behind. That works until it doesn't: when the team grows, requirements shift, or license costs get questioned at renewal.

This guide is for teams making this decision deliberately — whether for the first time or reassessing an existing setup. What authoring tools do, which categories exist, what the relevant tools cost, and which questions to ask before committing.

## What Is an E-Learning Authoring Tool?

An authoring tool is software for building e-learning courses. It lets you assemble learning pages, add interactions, design quiz questions, and incorporate multimedia — without needing programming skills.

The output can be deployed in several ways:

- As a **SCORM package** uploaded to an LMS (the traditional route)
- As an **xAPI file** that tracks learning activity in more detail
- As a **direct link** via the authoring tool's own hosting
- Integrated into a **platform** that combines authoring and LMS

Authoring tools are not the same as an LMS. The authoring tool is the kitchen — that's where the course gets built. The LMS is the dining room — that's where it gets served and consumed. Some platforms combine both; most traditional solutions keep them separate.

## Do You Actually Need an Authoring Tool?

Not every team does. Before evaluating, it's worth asking whether a specialized authoring tool is the right approach at all.

**An authoring tool makes sense when:**

- You build courses that require complex interactions, branching, or software simulations
- You deliver SCORM packages to multiple external LMS platforms
- You have a dedicated team producing courses regularly
- You need full control over course design and branding

**An authoring tool is likely overkill when:**

- You primarily deliver informational content (text, video, short quizzes)
- You train internally and control the LMS yourself
- Your team has no instructional design background
- You produce 2–5 courses per year, not 20–50

In these cases, an LMS with a built-in course editor is often the leaner solution — less complexity, lower license costs, less friction between tools.

  don't need a specialized authoring tool like Articulate Storyline — and you
  shouldn't be paying for one.
</Callout>

## The Four Main Categories

### Desktop Authoring Tools (SCORM-oriented)

Traditional tools like **Articulate Storyline**, **Adobe Captivate**, and **Lectora** are installed locally and produce SCORM or xAPI packages. Powerful, but with a steep learning curve and an assumed instructional design background.

For who: Teams with dedicated ID experts building complex courses for multiple LMS platforms.

### Browser-Based Authoring Tools

**Articulate Rise**, **Easygenerator**, **Lectora Online**, and similar tools run in the browser, enable team collaboration, and export SCORM or HTML5. Simpler than desktop tools, but less powerful for complex interactions.

For who: Teams working collaboratively who need simpler course formats.

### AI-Native Authoring Tools

Newer vendors like **Coursebox** integrate AI as a core function — not an add-on. Course structure, text, and quiz questions emerge from briefs or source documents. Higher production speed, lower design control. More detail in the [AI authoring tools guide](/en/blog/ki-autorentools-ratgeber).

For who: Teams prioritizing volume who want AI-generated drafts as a starting point.

### Integrated Platforms (LMS + Authoring)

**Scibly** and similar solutions don't separate authoring from delivery. You build the course in the same system where it gets delivered — no export, no upload step.

For who: SMBs and mid-market teams without an instructional design background who want to create and deploy training internally.

## The Major Tools

### Articulate 360

The industry standard. Includes two main tools: **Storyline** (desktop, powerful, complex) and **Rise** (browser-based, simpler, less flexible). Plus templates, collaboration features, and an AI assistant.

- **Price:** ≈$1,299/author/year
- **Strengths:** Largest community, most extensive template library, reliable SCORM export, continuous development
- **Weaknesses:** Expensive for small teams, Storyline learning curve is real, AI features still feel fragmented
- **Right for:** Experienced instructional designers, teams delivering complex productions

### iSpring Suite

PowerPoint as the starting point. Teams that currently build courses in PowerPoint can use iSpring as a direct upgrade path.

- **Price:** ≈$770/author/year
- **Strengths:** Shallow learning curve, solid PPT compatibility, good dialogue simulations, cheaper than Articulate
- **Weaknesses:** Courses look like PowerPoint, limited design freedom
- **Right for:** Teams digitizing existing PPT content, beginners without an ID background

### Adobe Captivate

Long the first choice for software simulations. More polarizing since its redesign.

- **Price:** ≈$33/month (Adobe CC)
- **Strengths:** Strong click-through simulations, responsive design
- **Weaknesses:** Steep learning curve, shrinking community, UX criticism post-redesign
- **Right for:** Teams primarily building software training with realistic UI simulations

### Lectora

Corporate standard in government and regulated industries. Focus on accessibility and deep SCORM/xAPI control.

- **Price:** ≈$1,299/author/year (Lectora Online)
- **Strengths:** WCAG-compliant, deep technical control, long market presence
- **Weaknesses:** Dated interface, slow workflow, limited adoption outside corporate/government
- **Right for:** Large organizations with strict accessibility and compliance requirements

### H5P

Open source, free, integrates with Moodle and WordPress.

- **Price:** Free (self-hosted) / from ≈$79/month (H5P.com)
- **Strengths:** No license costs, 50+ interactive content types, direct Moodle integration
- **Weaknesses:** Not a complete authoring tool — no course routing, no consistent course design across pages
- **Right for:** Moodle users, teams embedding interactive elements into existing pages

### Easygenerator

Browser-based, collaborative, built for simplicity. Positioned for subject matter experts without an ID background.

- **Price:** ≈$900/author/year
- **Strengths:** Very shallow learning curve, good collaboration features, integrated AI support
- **Weaknesses:** Limited design flexibility, less powerful than Articulate
- **Right for:** Teams where subject matter experts in business departments create courses themselves, not a central ID team

### Scibly

Not a standalone authoring tool — connects course creation with the LMS in one system.

- **Price:** Usage-based, [transparent on scibly.io](/en)
- **Strengths:** No SCORM export needed, integrated AI workflow, fast deployment
- **Weaknesses:** Not suited for external course delivery, no complex ID productions
- **Right for:** SMBs and mid-market, internal use without dedicated IT or ID support

## What Makes the Right Tool for Your Team

Five criteria that should feature in every evaluation:

**1. Who builds the courses?** Experienced instructional designers can use Storyline's full power. HR generalists need a tool that works without an ID background.

**2. Where do courses go?** Multiple external LMS platforms → SCORM export is mandatory. Internal LMS you control → integrated platforms become attractive.

**3. How complex are your courses?** Simple information modules: Rise, Easygenerator, integrated platforms. Complex branching, simulations: Storyline, Captivate.

**4. How many courses per year?** Few (1–5): maturity and simplicity matter more than production speed. Many (20+): AI support and fast workflows make the difference.

**5. What's the budget per author?** Under $500/year: H5P, Scibly, Easygenerator starter. $500–$1,000: iSpring, Easygenerator, Coursebox. Over $1,000: Articulate, Lectora.

## The Right Evaluation Sequence

1. **Decide the deployment path first.** SCORM external or LMS-internal? This immediately eliminates half the options.
2. **Build a shortlist of at most 3 tools.** More creates more confusion than clarity.
3. **Test with real content.** No demo course with placeholder text — use an actual source document from your context.
4. **Involve the actual course builders.** The person making the decision and the person using the tool daily are often not the same.
5. **Calculate total costs.** Authoring tool + LMS + implementation + training = actual first-year investment.

<Callout type="warning">
  Don't buy a tool based on a vendor demo. Everything works in controlled demos.
  Insist on a 14-day trial and build a real course during that time — then you
  know what you're buying.
</Callout>
