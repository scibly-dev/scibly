Prior knowledge is the single most important factor in which learning method works best for a given person. The same training can be exactly right for beginners and too slow or redundant for experienced colleagues, and vice versa. The research shows this especially clearly when comparing problem-solving before instruction to instruction before problem-solving: with low prior knowledge, the first path has an advantage for near-transfer tasks; with high prior knowledge, the second does.

## Why "one training for everyone" is rarely optimal

Most corporate training is built for an imagined average person, even though real participant groups are rarely homogeneous. New employees with no prior experience at all often sit in the same course as experienced colleagues who already know parts of the topic. For one group, the course is too slow and redundant; for the other, too fast and full of gaps. Both effects reduce actual learning outcomes, for opposite reasons.

## How to account for prior knowledge in training design

| Prior knowledge level | Recommended approach                                                                               |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low (novices)         | Worked examples, clear structure, guided rather than open-ended tasks                              |
| Medium                | Brief refresher, then a quick transition to independent practice                                   |
| High (experienced)    | Jump straight into practice, explanations only for genuine gaps, no repeating what's already known |

The practical lever is rarely a completely separate course per level, but branched entry points: a short placement test or self-assessment at the start that decides where someone enters the course.

## The expertise reversal effect

A particularly practice-relevant finding is the so-called expertise reversal effect: learning material optimized for beginners, for example with many explanatory intermediate steps, can actually slow down learning progress for experienced people, because it directs time and attention toward things they already know. That's why a single onboarding course identical for everyone often doesn't really fit either new or experienced employees.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can prior knowledge be assessed before a training program?

A short placement test or an honest self-assessment at the start is often enough to roughly distinguish between low, medium, and high prior knowledge. The effort for this is small compared to the benefit of a better-matched entry point.

### What is the expertise reversal effect?

It describes how learning material that's helpful for novices, such as very detailed explanations, can actually slow down learning progress for people with existing knowledge, because it spends time on things they already know.

### Does a completely separate course need to be built for every prior-knowledge level?

Not necessarily. Often a single course with branched entry points is enough, letting experienced people skip certain foundational sections instead of creating a completely separate version.

### Does this also apply to compliance training everyone has to take?

Yes, differentiation is worthwhile there too, for example by letting people skip already-familiar sections via a placement test, while the actually new or risk-relevant part stays the same for everyone.
