A worked example shows a complete solution step by step before learners attempt a similar task on their own. Instead of "here is the problem, solve it," the message is "here is how an expert solves this problem, now try a similar one." The technique traces back to John Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory and is one of the most thoroughly studied instructional design techniques in existence.

## A concrete example from practice

When onboarding a new hire to a CRM system, a manager first demonstrates on screen, step by step, how to correctly create a new lead, categorize it, and assign it to a sales rep, explaining the reasoning behind each click. Only afterward does the new employee handle a similar, but not identical, case on their own. The difference from "just let them try it": the cognitive capacity that would otherwise go into trial and error is instead available for understanding the process itself.

## What the research shows

A meta-analysis of worked examples in mathematics education, covering 55 studies and 181 effects, found a moderate advantage over less guided learning formats (effect size g = 0.48). The effect was larger for correct, complete examples than for flawed or incomplete ones. A refinement of the technique, called subgoal labeling, further breaks an example into named sub-steps, such as "Step 1: verify the lead source" instead of one continuous sequence without intermediate headers. Studies in introductory programming courses show that subgoal labels are associated with lower dropout rates.

<Callout type="tip">
  Worked examples work best for novices and complex, unfamiliar material. For
  more advanced learners, the effect can partially reverse, a well-documented
  phenomenon called the expertise reversal effect: someone who already knows a
  procedure benefits more from independent practice than from another
  demonstration.
</Callout>
